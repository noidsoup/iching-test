# Web synthesis — I Ching overview

One-paragraph summary: Consolidated notes from the English Wikipedia article on the I Ching and cross-checks with standard sinological accounts of the Zhou yi core, Han canonization, manuscript finds, and global reception. This page is a **synthesis for the project wiki**, not a reproduction of Wikipedia text.

## Key points

- The **Classic of Changes** (易經 *Yìjīng*, often romanized **I Ching**) is built on a much older core, the **Zhou Changes** (周易 *Zhōuyì*), a Western Zhou–period divination manual whose language has been compared to dated bronze inscriptions.
- Modern scholarship places the stabilization of the core text roughly between the **10th and 4th centuries BCE**; Edward Shaughnessy has argued for a **late 9th century BCE** horizon tied to the reign of King Xuan of Zhou (debated like all dating of composite texts).
- During the **Warring States** and early empire, the work accumulated philosophical commentaries known collectively as the **Ten Wings** (易傳 *Yì zhuàn*), written in a later stratum of Chinese than the Zhou yi proper; traditional ascription to Confucius is **not accepted** as historical fact today.
- In **136 BCE**, Emperor Wu of Han elevated the Zhou yi + commentaries among the **Five Classics**; the canonized whole became the **I Ching** as imperial orthodoxy for two millennia, later joined by alternate witnesses from archaeology.
- The received **King Wen sequence** of 64 hexagrams is **not** the only ancient order: the **Mawangdui** silk manuscripts (Western Han tomb, sealed 168 BCE) use a different arrangement (eight groups by upper trigram).
- The **Rites of Zhou** triad **Lianshan / Guicang / Zhouyi** hints at alternate early Yi books; only Zhouyi survived integrally in the imperial line (see [[three-yi-tradition-zhouli]]).
- Interpretation traditions split broadly between **image–number** (象數) and **meaning–principle** (義理) approaches; **Wang Bi** (3rd century CE) and later **Zhu Xi** (Song) reshaped how literati read the same graphs.

## Detailed notes

### Structure of the Zhou yi layer

The basic figure is the **hexagram** (卦 *guà*): six stacked lines, each **yin** (broken) or **yang** (solid). The received Zhou yi gives **64** hexagrams, each with a **name**, a **hexagram statement** (彖 / judgment layer in received editions), and **six line statements** (爻辭 *yáocí*). Line tags use numbers **6** and **9** for changing lines and **7** and **8** for stable lines in later reconstructions of practice.

The opening formula **元亨利貞** (*yuán hēng lì zhēn*) recurs and already attracted exegetical attention by the late first millennium BCE.

### Divination in history

Early usage is attested in **Zuo Zhuan** and **Guoyu** narratives (royal consultations, 7th–5th centuries BCE). By the Qin period, private consultation appears in excavated administrative contexts (e.g. Shuihudi parallels in scholarship). **Yarrow** methods are described in mature form in the **Great Commentary** (*Xì cí* 系辭); **Zhu Xi** reconstructed a yarrow procedure still common in East Asia. **Coin** methods spread more widely from the Tang onward.

### Han canon and Ten Wings

The **Ten Wings** bundle the **Tuan**, **Xiang**, **Wényán**, **Xì cí**, **Shuō guà**, **Xù guà**, and **Zá guà** traditions (upper/lower splits where applicable). The **Great Commentary** (~300 BCE in scholarly dating) frames the classic as a **microcosm** of change and connects trigram cosmology to ethics. Canonization bound these wings to the oracle text for Confucian state ideology.

### Manuscript revolution (20th century)

Important witnesses include:

- **Mawangdui** (1973): silk **Yijing** with alternate hexagram order and textual variants.
- **Shanghai Museum Chu bamboo Zhou yi** (purchased 1994; Warring States Chu copy, ~**300 BCE** C-14 band in published studies): shows the Zhou yi circulating close to the received form while still exhibiting **small textual variation** across manuscripts.

These finds fuel the **“modernist school”** of readings that compare received text against Western Zhou and Warring States witnesses (see [[edward-shaughnessy]], [[mawangdui-silk-manuscripts]], [[shanghai-museum-chu-bamboo-zhouyi]]).

### Reception beyond China

Brief landmarks useful for cultural context: Jesuit diagrams reached **Leibniz** (binary arithmetic correspondence, 1703 essay); **Richard Wilhelm**’s German translation (1924) and Cary Baynes’ English version shaped 20th-century European and American engagement; **Carl Jung**’s foreword framed the book for depth psychology; East Asian Confucian and Buddhist lineages produced enormous commentary corpora (Korea, Japan, Vietnam).
